# How to Build an Intelligence Requirements Framework for Fast-Moving Crises

Public sources from 2023 through 2026 point to a shared practitioner finding: crisis intelligence works better when requirements are tied to decisions, owners, collection questions, review cycles, and coordination rules. That finding does not prove a single doctrine for governments, NGOs, infrastructure operators, or global leaders. It does support one defensible thesis: in a fast-moving crisis, the starting problem is not simply scarce data. It is the failure to convert urgent choices into prioritized questions that can guide collection, analysis, dissemination, and updates.

### Key Findings

- Requirements-driven intelligence starts with decisions, not available feeds.
- GIR, PIR, and SIR language can keep broad concerns, operational questions, and collection tasks aligned.
- Stakeholder input is described as ongoing, with review and feedback shaping requirements.
- A compact matrix can connect requirements, indicators, sources, collection windows, and decision points.
- Information integrity and data responsibility belong inside requirements design, not after collection.
- The evidence supports a practical framework, but not a full crisis operating model for every sector.

## Finding 1: Start with the decision, not the data feed

An intelligence requirements framework should begin with the decision that leaders need to make. Flashpoint’s 2026 explainer states the requirements-driven shift plainly: the question changes from what data can be obtained to what decisions need to be made. Analyst1 makes the same point in practical terms: a requirement should be tied to a decision, and if the intelligence would not change a decision or action, the requirement may not justify production effort.

That is the first design rule for forensic crisis intelligence. Do not start by listing every available source. Start by naming the choice, the decision owner, and what new information would change the next action. Ontic’s description of the planning and direction phase supports this sequencing. It says the phase defines what needs protection and uses specific, stakeholder-shaped questions to drive collection.

### How We Know

This article synthesizes the supplied public sources dated 2023 to 2026, including intelligence-requirements explainers, a 2024 Army article on refining PIRs, emergency-management work on information integrity, and a 2025 rapid evidence review on crisis data sharing. The method is interpretive: it compares recurring concepts across sources, then narrows them into a crisis requirements framework.

The limits matter. Several sources are cyber or vendor-adjacent, and the packet does not provide a full civil-protection, humanitarian, or infrastructure-operator doctrine. The evidence supports decision-linked requirements and reviewable collection questions. It does not prove that one template fits every crisis.

A usable first pass asks:

- What decision is pending?
- Who owns it?
- What answer would change action?
- What assumption needs testing?
- When should the requirement be reviewed?

## Finding 2: Use a GIR, PIR, and SIR hierarchy to keep strategy, operations, and collection aligned

The GIR, PIR, and SIR hierarchy gives crisis teams a shared way to move from broad concern to answerable collection. Flashpoint defines General Intelligence Requirements as the broad why, Priority Intelligence Requirements as the operational what, and Specific Intelligence Requirements as the tactical how. The article is written for cyber contexts, so its sector examples should not be copied into unrelated crises. The structure, however, is useful as a vocabulary for decision-grade analysis.

A GIR frames the broad concern leaders need to understand. A PIR turns that concern into a high-impact question that can guide monitoring or escalation. A SIR breaks the PIR into specific observations or answers that collectors and analysts can pursue. The Army article on refining PIRs adds an important discipline: PIRs need indicators and SIRs detailed enough that collectors or analysts know what would satisfy the requirement.

For governments, NGOs, and infrastructure operators, the safe adaptation is not to borrow military examples. It is to write each layer in language that decision owners and collectors can act on. A vague PIR may sound strategic, but it leaves collection open-ended. A well-shaped PIR makes the decision point visible. A useful SIR then states what must be observed, from which source type, within what collection window, and for which decision.

The hierarchy also helps prevent a common planning error supported by the sources: treating requirements as static. Flashpoint describes PIRs as dynamic, and Dataminr says requirements should be reviewed on a schedule that fits operational needs and threat velocity. In a crisis, that implies a short, living hierarchy rather than a long document that no one revisits.

> “GIR/PIR/SIR hierarchy is not jargon; it is a translation mechanism. GIRs hold strategic concern, PIRs expose operational decisions, and SIRs define observable indicators, so collection and dissemination do not drift apart.”

## Finding 3: Prioritize requirements through ownership, stakeholder review, and decision impact

Prioritization is not just ranking questions by interest. The supplied sources support three practical tests: ownership, stakeholder review, and decision impact. Analyst1 says requirements drive dissemination when every product is tied to a requirement and every requirement has an owner. That matters because the answer should go to the stakeholder who needs it, in a form that supports the decision it was meant to inform.

Stakeholder engagement is also described as recurring work. Analyst1 calls requirement collection a process, not a one-time exercise, and points to regular touchpoints about current information needs, upcoming decisions, and whether previous intelligence was useful. ThreatConnect similarly describes reviewing draft requirements with stakeholders through recurring meetings or survey-style feedback, then finalizing requirements based on that input.

Intel 471 adds a useful prioritization mechanism. It describes recording PIRs in a master PIR Collection Register, then scoring, ranking, and combining them into collection guidance. The article need not invent a scoring model to use that idea. A compact crisis version can rank requirements by decision relevance, owner, timing, and collection feasibility, because those elements appear across the accepted sources.

### Strongest objection and reply

The strongest objection is that a requirements framework can feel too slow during a fast-moving crisis. The evidence-based reply is not to make the framework heavier. Umbrex warns that formal requirements documents can feel bureaucratic if overengineered and frames the artifact as a living set of prioritized questions. Dataminr also supports review and evolution over time.

The practical answer is compactness: assign an owner, state the decision, write the PIR, break it into SIRs, and schedule review. That is enough structure to reduce unfocused collection without pretending that a crisis allows perfect planning.

## Evidence asset: Build a compact crisis requirements matrix

A crisis requirements matrix should be treated as a planning artifact, not a universal doctrine. The supported fields come from Umbrex’s matrix guidance and the Army article’s information collection matrix: requirement, why it matters, priority level, decision owner, timing, indicators, sources, collection method, PIRs, SIRs, decision points, collection time windows, and available collection capabilities.

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Requirement | The decision-linked question that needs an answer |
| Why it matters | The decision or action the answer may affect |
| Owner | The stakeholder responsible for using the answer |
| Priority and timing | Ranking, deadline, and review point |
| PIR and SIR | The operational question and the specific collection questions |
| Indicators | Observable signs that would satisfy or challenge the requirement |
| Sources and method | Where information may come from and how it will be collected |
| Assumptions | Beliefs that should be tested or revisited |
| Decision point | The point at which the answer should inform action |
| Collection window | The time window in which collection is useful |

The matrix should reveal overlap, missing topics, hidden assumptions, and unrealistic collection demands before collection begins. Umbrex explicitly recommends reviewing requirements for overlap, missing topics, hidden assumptions, and unrealistic collection demands. The Army article similarly emphasizes linking PIRs, indicators, SIRs, decision points, collection time windows, and capabilities.

For forensic crisis intelligence, the value is traceability. The matrix preserves the link between decision, requirement, collection question, assumption, and review point. It also helps separate what is known from what is merely being watched. That distinction is a practical guardrail when leaders are asking for answers faster than sources can support them.

## Finding 4: Treat coordination, information integrity, and data responsibility as requirements-design issues

Crisis intelligence requirements should specify not only what information is needed, but also who can collect, access, share, and act on it. Carnegie’s 2023 emergency-management article frames information integrity as a coordination problem across multiple stakeholders during crisis response. It also notes that crises may require broad engagement from individuals, households, organizations, and various levels of government.

That matters for requirements design because mismatched expectations can arise when governments and private actors do not clearly communicate plans and needs. Carnegie describes situations in which private actors must explain their plans to governments, while unclear government needs can create mismatches. A requirements framework cannot solve every coordination problem, but it can make information needs, owners, and review points explicit.

The 2025 Scientific Advice Mechanism rapid evidence review adds the data-responsibility layer. Its excerpt refers to secure, privacy-preserving, rapid data sharing between emergency services, NGOs, and governments. It also cites humanitarian data responsibility practices such as data-sharing agreements, role-based access, protection measures, data protection impact assessments, consent challenges, and fair processing for vulnerable subjects.

### What Changes on Monday

Operationally, the next revision of a crisis requirements document should add two fields that teams sometimes leave implicit: access and sharing. For each PIR or SIR, ask who is allowed to collect the information, who may receive it, and what protection measure is required before sharing.

## Conclusion

The defensible method is simple: name the decision, assign an owner, write the PIR, break it into SIRs and indicators, identify sources and collection windows, record assumptions, define review points, and protect information integrity. The evidence does not support an all-purpose doctrine for every crisis actor. It does support a compact, stakeholder-owned, iterative framework for decision-grade analysis.

Use the framework as a checklist to test whether current crisis intelligence work is tied to decisions, owners, collection questions, assumptions, and review triggers before the next fast-moving event.
